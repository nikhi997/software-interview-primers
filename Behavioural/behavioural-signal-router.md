# Behavioural Signal Router: choosing the right story under pressure

*[← Chapter 10](chapter-10/behavioural-chapter-10.md) · [Contents](behavioural-README.md)*

The chapters teach the signals. The appendix lets you look them up. This router is for the moment in the room when a question lands and you need to decide, quickly, which story to play. The mock deck rehearses that decision under pressure.

Use this as a routing aid, not a prophecy. Behavioural questions are **probable**, not deterministic: the same surface question can be fishing for different evidence depending on the interviewer, the role, and the follow-up.

> 💡 **Concept notes — route before you answer**
> Don't ask, "Which story do I want to tell?" Ask, "What signal are they probably testing, and what follow-up would prove it?" Then pick the story that gives evidence for that signal. If the follow-up shifts the probe, shift the emphasis of the story.

---

## First: keep the bank private

These companion tools are generic on purpose. Your real story bank should live in a private or ignored file, not in committed repository content. When you write and rehearse your own stories:

- anonymize employers, teams, systems, customers, and people;
- respect NDA boundaries and leave out confidential implementation details;
- never invent numbers — use true metrics, honest estimates, or concrete non-numeric evidence;
- never overclaim your contribution in a team effort; credit others and make your own actions clear.

---

## The routing chain

Run this chain before you start speaking:

1. **Paraphrase what you heard.** Is the prompt about disagreement, failure, ownership, unclear work, pressure, pride, or motivation?
2. **Name the likely family.** Map it to the Chapter 4–7 families, or to opener / proudest project / motivation.
3. **Pick primary and secondary signals.** Primary is what the answer must prove. Secondary is the signal that often rides along.
4. **Listen for the disambiguating clue.** The first follow-up usually reveals the real probe.
5. **Select a story by evidence, not drama.** Pick the story with the cleanest actions, tradeoff, outcome, and follow-up depth.
6. **Reject the wrong story.** A flashy story aimed at the wrong signal is worse than a modest story that answers the probe.

## Router table

| What you hear | Likely family | Probable primary signal | Probable secondary signal | Disambiguating clue | Choose a story that shows... | Wrong story for this route |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Tell me about yourself." / "Walk me through your background." | Opener | Communication | Motivation / ownership | Do they ask "why this role?" or "which project shaped you most?" | A 60–90 second arc: what you do, one or two proof points, and why this role fits. | A full life history, a resume recitation, or a technical deep dive with no role fit. |
| "What project are you proudest of?" | Proudest project | Impact | Ownership / communication | "Why are you proud?" tests values; "what did you do?" tests ownership; "how do you know it worked?" tests impact. | A real contribution with a clear before/after, your actions, and evidence of impact. | The most technically complex project if you cannot explain why it mattered or what you personally did. |
| "Why this company / role?" / "What are you looking for next?" | Motivation | Communication | Growth / ownership | Follow-ups ask whether your reasons connect to the actual role, not a generic brand. | A specific, honest match between your direction, the role's work, and the kind of growth you want. | Flattery, generic prestige, compensation-first answers, or claims that could fit any company. |
| "Tell me about a disagreement with a teammate." | Conflict | Collaboration / conflict | Communication / growth | If they ask "what did they think?" they want empathy. If they ask "how was it resolved?" they want the mechanism. | You steelmanning the other view, arguing on merits, and resolving constructively. | "I was right and they eventually agreed," especially if the other person sounds foolish. |
| "A time you disagreed with your manager." | Conflict with power dynamic | Collaboration / conflict | Judgment / communication | The probe is whether you push back respectfully and know when to defer. | Direct, respectful escalation, evidence, and either changed minds or committed support after the decision. | Going around the manager, venting, or framing the manager as the villain. |
| "A time you convinced someone" / "received pushback." | Conflict / influence | Collaboration / conflict | Ownership | If they ask "why were they hesitant?" they are testing whether you understood resistance. | You identifying the concern, using data or a prototype, and making it easier to say yes. | A story where persuasion means repeating yourself louder or using status. |
| "A conflict with another team." | Conflict across boundaries | Collaboration / conflict | Ownership / communication | Listen for "shared goal" language: they want maturity across incentives. | Shared goals, neutral framing, tradeoff clarity, and a resolution that preserves the relationship. | A turf-war story where your team wins and the other team is blamed. |
| "Tell me about your biggest failure." | Failure | Growth / self-awareness | Ownership | If they ask "what did you change?" the lesson is the whole answer. | A real, owned mistake, recovery, and a concrete behavior that changed afterward. | A fake weakness, a humblebrag, or a disaster with no recovery and no learning. |
| "A time you made a mistake" / "broke something." | Failure | Growth / self-awareness | Communication under pressure | Follow-ups about who was told and when test accountability. | Immediate ownership, damage control, transparent communication, and a lasting safeguard. | A blame-shift story where the system, requirements, or another person did it. |
| "Critical feedback you received." | Feedback | Growth / self-awareness | Collaboration | If they ask "how did you react at first?" they are testing honesty, not perfection. | Feedback that stung, what you changed, and evidence the change showed up later. | Feedback that is secretly praise or an answer where you explain why the feedback was wrong. |
| "Tell me about a time you showed leadership." | Leadership | Ownership / drive | Collaboration / impact | If they ask "what authority did you have?" they want influence without authority. | You noticing unowned pain, taking responsibility, bringing people along, and following through. | A title-based story: "I was the lead, so people did what I said." |
| "A time you took initiative" / "went beyond your role." | Leadership / initiative | Ownership / drive | Impact | The clue is whether you acted before being asked and drove it to done. | The "I noticed X, so I did Y" spine, with a result and follow-up. | A task assigned to you, or a nice idea you started but never landed. |
| "A time you drove a project from start to finish." | Leadership / ownership | Ownership / drive | Communication / impact | Follow-ups about coordination test whether you handled the messy middle. | Planning, alignment, risk handling, execution, and a clear outcome. | Only the build phase, with no alignment, tradeoff, stakeholder, or result evidence. |
| "A time you influenced without authority." | Leadership / influence | Ownership / drive | Collaboration / conflict | If they ask "why did people follow?" they want the influence mechanism. | Data, prototype, trust, coalition-building, and generous credit. | "I convinced everyone" with no mechanism or resistance. |
| "Unclear requirements" / "decision without all the information." | Ambiguity | Ambiguity | Communication | The clue is whether you made assumptions visible and reversible. | Unknowns named, cheap uncertainty reduction, explicit assumptions, small learning step, iteration. | Waiting for perfect clarity, or charging ahead without alignment. |
| "Competing priorities" / "had to say no." | Prioritization | Ambiguity / judgment | Impact / communication | They will ask "why that choice?" and "who agreed?" | A basis for prioritization: impact, risk, blockers, effort, deadline, and stakeholder alignment. | "I just worked harder" or "I did everything." |
| "Tight deadline" / "project was slipping." | Deadline pressure | Communication under pressure | Ownership / ambiguity | If they ask "when did you tell people?" they are testing early escalation. | You spotted risk early, came with options, cut scope or renegotiated honestly, and protected trust. | A silent miss, a last-minute surprise, or heroics as the only solution. |
| "Had to learn something quickly." | Ambiguity / growth | Ambiguity | Growth / ownership | The follow-up tests whether learning produced delivery, not just effort. | You identified the minimum needed, learned by doing, asked focused help, and shipped responsibly. | A story about consuming resources with no decision, delivery, or impact. |
| "Kept a remote team aligned" / "worked asynchronously." | Remote/async collaboration | Communication | Ownership / collaboration | "How did you know they agreed?" tests whether a broadcast became shared understanding. | Durable context, owners, decision state, response windows, equitable handoffs, and focused synchronous discussion only where needed. | More meetings, presence surveillance, or expecting instant replies across time zones. |
| "Different cultures or communication styles." | Cross-cultural collaboration | Collaboration / conflict | Self-awareness | "What assumption did you make?" tests whether you examined your interpretation. | Observable behavior, respectful inquiry, reciprocal adaptation, and working norms that let multiple styles contribute. | Stereotypes, diagnosing intent, or requiring only the other person to assimilate. |
| "Navigated a reorg or major change." | Change / ambiguity | Ambiguity / adaptability | Ownership / communication | "What was actually known?" separates responsible action from rumor and false reassurance. | Facts vs unknowns, reset commitments, continuity/handoffs, empathy, and useful action inside your control. | Badmouthing, speculation, confidential personnel detail, or "stayed positive" with no concrete action. |
| "Another team was a critical dependency." | Cross-team dependency | Collaboration / ownership | Prioritization | "What pressure were they under?" tests whether you saw shared constraints rather than a blocker. | Shared outcome, explicit interface/owner/date, mitigation options, neutral risk escalation, and reduced future coupling. | Blame, surprise escalation, repeated chasing with no contract, or silent dependency risk. |
| "Found a security/privacy risk." | Risk / integrity | Judgment / ownership | Communication | "What did you do first?" tests containment, evidence handling, and approved escalation. | Containment, need-to-know reporting, proportionate options, expert partnership, verification, and a systemic guardrail. | Probing production further, copying sensitive data, hiding for schedule, or guessing legal impact. |
| "Responsible AI concern." | Product risk / ambiguity | Judgment / ambiguity | Ownership / collaboration | "Who could be harmed?" tests whether the analysis starts with people and outcomes, not a model metric. | Defined harms, relevant slice/failure evaluation, human accountability/appeal, monitoring, and a stop or no-build condition. | "The model is objective," aggregate accuracy only, disclaimer-only mitigation, or no accountable human. |
| "Disagreed with legal/compliance/audit." | Governed disagreement | Collaboration / judgment | Communication | "What requirement was behind the control?" tests curiosity about obligation and risk. | Underlying obligation, technical evidence, compliant alternatives, decision authority, residual risk, and recorded outcome. | Calling partners bureaucracy, shipping around them, or claiming legal expertise you do not have. |
| "Pressured to take an unsafe shortcut." | Integrity under pressure | Integrity / judgment | Ownership / communication | "What would you do if ordered?" tests boundary, escalation, and constructive alternatives. | Concrete harm, calm refusal, minimum safe path, appropriate escalation/documentation, and verified outcome. | Quiet compliance, vague objection, grandstanding, or refusal with no safe alternative. |

## Ambiguous prompts: let the follow-up reveal the probe

Some prompts are deliberately broad. Don't lock onto the first signal too early. Answer with a story that can flex, and listen for where the interviewer digs.

| Ambiguous question | Could be probing... | Follow-up that reveals it | How to pivot |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Tell me about a hard project." | Impact, ambiguity, ownership, conflict, or growth | "What made it hard?" | If the hardness was unclear goals, emphasize assumptions. If it was people, emphasize collaboration. If it was failure, emphasize learning. |
| "Tell me about a time something didn't go as planned." | Failure, deadline pressure, ambiguity | "What was your role in it?" / "When did you realize?" | Own your part, then choose whether the answer is about recovery and learning or early communication and scope tradeoffs. |
| "Tell me about a time you changed someone's mind." | Conflict, influence, leadership | "Why were they against it?" | Steelman the resistance first. Then show the influence mechanism: data, prototype, test, or shared goal. |
| "What's a project you're proud of?" | Impact, ownership, communication, values | "What did you personally do?" / "How do you know it mattered?" | Keep the story anchored on your actions and evidence, not the project's brand or size. |
| "Tell me about a time you handled pressure." | Deadline, failure, ambiguity, communication | "Who did you tell?" / "What options did you consider?" | Move away from endurance. Show judgment: options, tradeoffs, early communication, and calm execution. |
| "Tell me about a difficult teammate." | Conflict, collaboration, self-awareness | "What was your part in it?" | Stay neutral. Describe the working mismatch, your actions, and what improved. Do not diagnose or attack the person. |
| "What would your manager say you need to improve?" | Growth, communication, humility | "What have you done about it?" | Name real feedback, your concrete change, and how you know the change is visible. |
| "Tell me about a values conflict." | Security/privacy, responsible AI, compliance, or ordinary disagreement | "Who could be harmed?" / "What obligation applied?" | Do not force an ethics story onto a routine preference. Name the affected party, evidence, authority, and safe alternatives only when they are genuinely present. |

## Story-selection criteria

Before you commit to a story, check it quickly:

- **Signal fit:** does the story prove the probable primary signal without forcing it?
- **Your action:** can the interviewer list what you personally did?
- **Evidence:** is there a true number, honest estimate, before/after, scope, risk reduced, or person unblocked?
- **Tradeoff:** can you explain why you chose one path over another?
- **Follow-up depth:** can the story survive "why?" twice?
- **Tone:** can you tell it without blaming, badmouthing, or overclaiming?

If any answer is no, pick another card.

## Wrong-story warnings by signal

| Signal being tested | Stories that look tempting but are wrong |
|---|---|
| Collaboration / conflict | Stories where the other person is a caricature, the only lesson is that you were right, or the resolution is "they gave in." |
| Growth / self-awareness | Fake failures, blame-heavy failures, or stories where the lesson is vague: "I learned to be more careful." |
| Ownership / drive | Assigned tasks with no extra ownership, idea-only stories with no finish, or stories where authority did all the work. |
| Ambiguity | Stories where requirements were actually clear, where you waited passively, or where you acted without stating assumptions. |
| Impact | Technically impressive work with no evidence it mattered, or numbers you cannot defend. |
| Communication | Answers that require a long backstory, hide the result, or sound memorized instead of conversational. |
| Integrity / risk judgment | Stories that dramatize preference as danger, conceal real harm, route around governance, expose sensitive detail, or refuse without a viable safe next step. |

---

## The bumper sticker

> *A behavioural prompt is a routing problem before it is a storytelling problem. Hear the probable signal, wait for the clue, pick the story with the right evidence, and reject the shiny story that answers the wrong question.*
