# Chapter 8: The questions you ask

*[← Chapter 7](../chapter-07/behavioural-chapter-7.md) · [Contents](../behavioural-README.md)*

- [ ] **Mark as read**

Almost every interview ends with "so, what questions do you have for me?" — and most candidates treat it as the throwaway closing formality. It isn't. It's a graded part of the interview, it's the one stretch where *you* set the agenda, and it's your best window into whether this job is actually one you want. Asking nothing, or asking only "what's the team's tech stack," quietly costs you.

This chapter is short because the move is simple: come with **genuine, specific, thoughtful questions** that signal you're already thinking like a member of the team — and that you're evaluating *them* as much as they're evaluating you.

---

## Why this is graded

> 💡 **Concept notes — what your questions signal**
> Interviewers read your questions for: (1) **Genuine interest** — did you actually think about this role, or recycle generic questions? (2) **Seniority of thinking** — strong candidates ask about tradeoffs, challenges, and how decisions get made, not just perks and stack. (3) **What you value** — your questions reveal whether you care about impact, growth, craft, or just comp. (4) **Engagement** — a candidate with zero questions reads as uninterested or unprepared. The bar is low and most clear it poorly, so a few sharp questions are a cheap way to stand out — and end the interview on a high, memorable note.

---

## Questions that signal strength

Aim for questions that only make sense if you've thought seriously about the role and the work. Categories that land well:

- **About the actual work:** *"What's the most challenging technical problem the team is wrestling with right now?"* *"What does the path from idea to production look like here?"*
- **About how decisions get made:** *"When the team disagrees on a technical direction, how does that usually get resolved?"* *"How do you balance shipping fast against paying down tech debt?"*
- **About impact and priorities:** *"What would success in this role look like in the first six months?"* *"What's the most important thing the team needs to accomplish this year?"*
- **About growth:** *"How do engineers here grow toward senior/staff? What distinguishes the people who've done it?"*
- **About the team's reality, honestly:** *"What's something you'd change about how the team works if you could?"* *"What's been the hardest part of working here?"*

> 💡 **Concept notes — tailor to your interviewer**
> Match the question to who's in front of you. Ask an **engineer** about the codebase, the tech challenges, on-call, code review culture. Ask a **manager** about team priorities, growth paths, how they measure success, team health. Ask a **skip-level or director** about strategy, where the org is headed, how this team fits the bigger picture. Asking a line engineer about five-year strategy, or a director about code review nits, signals you didn't think about *who* you're talking to. Prepare a small pool and pick from it based on the room.

---

## Questions to avoid (in early rounds)

> 💡 **Concept notes — what not to lead with**
> Avoid, especially early: **easily-Googleable facts** ("what does the company do?") — signals zero prep. **Pure perks/comp/vacation** in a technical round — there's a time for that (with the recruiter, at offer stage), but leading with it in a tech interview signals misplaced priorities. **Empty flattery questions** ("what's the best thing about working here?") that invite a canned answer. **Nothing at all** — the single worst option. Save logistics and benefits for the recruiter; use technical and manager rounds for substance. You can always close with "I have more logistical questions I'll save for the recruiter" — which itself signals you know how the process works.

---

## Use the answers — for *you*

Beyond signaling, these questions are genuine due diligence. You're choosing where to spend the next several years; the answers to "what would you change?" and "what's been the hardest part?" tell you more about daily reality than any job description. Listen for evasiveness, for whether engineers seem energized or drained, for whether decisions sound collaborative or top-down. A behavioural round runs both directions — they're reading your signals, and you should be reading theirs.

> 💡 **Concept notes — keep a running list as you interview**
> As you go through rounds, jot down questions that occur to you about the *specific* team — something an engineer mentioned, a product decision you're curious about. Referencing something from earlier in the conversation ("you mentioned the migration earlier — what drove that decision?") is the strongest possible signal of genuine engagement, because it's impossible to fake. Keep three or four evergreen questions ready, but the best ones are born in the interview itself.

---

## Try it

1. Write five questions you'd genuinely want answered about a team you're interviewing with — and check that none are Googleable.
2. Sort them by who they're best aimed at: engineer, manager, or skip-level. Do you have at least one strong question for each?
3. Draft one question that demonstrates you've thought about the *challenges* of the role, not just its perks.
4. Practice the habit of noting a follow-up question mid-conversation, so you can reference something the interviewer said earlier.

## The bumper sticker

> *"Any questions for me?" is graded, not a formality. Come with specific, thoughtful questions tailored to who's in the room — about the work, the tradeoffs, and the team's reality. It signals genuine interest, ends on a high note, and tells *you* whether you actually want the job.*
