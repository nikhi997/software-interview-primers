# Chapter 3: Building your story bank

*[← Chapter 2](../chapter-02/behavioural-chapter-2.md) · [Contents](../behavioural-README.md)*

- [ ] **Mark as read**

Here's the secret that makes behavioural interviews manageable: you are not preparing for a hundred questions. You are preparing **eight to twelve stories**, each rich enough to be aimed at many questions. A single strong project — where you showed initiative, hit a conflict, made a mistake, and shipped real impact — can answer "tell me about leadership," "a conflict," "a failure," and "your proudest work," just by emphasizing a different facet each time.

The work of behavioural prep is *building that bank* and tagging each story with the signals it can deliver. Do this once, well, and you walk into every behavioural round with a deck of cards you can play against anything they ask.

---

## Why a bank beats memorizing answers

If you prepare answers question-by-question, you'll have fifty half-baked anecdotes and freeze when a question doesn't match one. If you prepare *stories*, you carry a small, deeply-known set you can flex on the spot.

> 💡 **Concept notes — stories are reusable, questions aren't**
> Most behavioural questions are the same handful of signals (Chapter 1) in different wording. So the right unit of preparation is the **story**, not the question. A well-built story is **multi-tag**: the same "I rescued the flaky deploy pipeline" story serves *initiative* (took unowned work), *conflict* (won over the skeptical senior), *impact* (cut deploy time), and *ambiguity* (no one had specified the fix). Aim for 8–12 stories whose combined tags cover every signal at least twice — then any question maps to a card you already hold.

---

## Mining your experience: the prompts

Stare at "tell me about your accomplishments" and your mind goes blank. So don't start there. Start with *specific* memory-jogging prompts and write raw notes for each — you'll polish later.

Walk your last few years and find a moment for each of these:

- A time you **shipped something** that mattered (your proudest impact).
- A time you **took initiative** no one asked you to take.
- A time you **disagreed** with someone — a peer, your manager, another team.
- A time you **failed**, broke something, or made a wrong call.
- A time you **got hard feedback** and changed because of it.
- A time you **worked through ambiguity** with no clear spec or direction.
- A time you **influenced people** without authority over them.
- A time you **missed or nearly missed a deadline**, and how you handled it.
- A time you **helped a teammate** or improved how the team worked.
- A time you **learned something hard**, fast.
- A time you **dealt with a difficult person** or a tense situation.
- The **hardest technical problem** you've solved, and how.

> 💡 **Concept notes — where to find stories if you feel you have none**
> Junior engineers and students often think they "haven't done anything interesting." You have. Stories live in: **course/side projects** (an architecture decision, a teammate who didn't pull weight), **internships** (any real task with a real outcome), **open source** (a PR debate, a maintainer disagreement), **clubs/teams/part-time jobs** (leadership and conflict are leadership and conflict regardless of domain), and **hackathons** (scope-cutting under extreme deadline pressure is a *great* ambiguity story). Impact doesn't have to be "saved the company a million dollars" — "made my four-person project team ship on time" is a real, scoreable story.

## The story-bank template

For each story, capture these fields (the full template is in the appendix). Writing them down is the work — it forces the specificity that vague mental recollection hides.

- **Title** — a short handle so you can find it fast ("Flaky deploy pipeline").
- **Situation & Task** — the setup and your charge, in 2–3 sentences.
- **Actions (the "I" list)** — the specific steps *you* took, in order. The longest field.
- **Result** — the outcome, with a **number** if at all possible, plus the lesson.
- **Signals it delivers** — tag it: initiative? conflict? impact? ambiguity? growth?
- **Likely follow-ups & your answers** — the "why did you...?" and "what would you do differently?" pre-thought-out.

> 💡 **Concept notes — the "what would you do differently?" field is mandatory**
> Every story needs a genuine, thoughtful answer to *"what would you do differently?"* — because it's the most common follow-up and the one that most reveals self-awareness. A bad answer ("nothing, it went great") signals no reflection. A good answer names a real, non-fatal improvement: *"I'd have brought the skeptical senior in earlier instead of showing up with a finished proposal — I created friction I didn't need to."* It should show growth without torpedoing the story's success. Write this field for *every* story before the interview.

## Choosing impact stories that travel

The best stories in your bank are the **multi-faceted** ones — a single rich experience with several signal-bearing moments. When you find one, mine it for all its angles:

> One project — say, *leading the migration of a monolith module to a service* — might contain: the **initiative** to propose it, a **conflict** with a teammate who wanted to wait, an **ambiguity** moment when requirements were unclear, a **mistake** (a rollout bug you caught), and a **quantified impact** (latency, reliability, team velocity). That's *five* questions answered by one deeply-known story. Prioritize building two or three of these "anchor" stories — they do the heaviest lifting.

Round out the bank with a few **single-purpose** stories for signals your anchors don't cover well — e.g., a clean "got hard feedback and changed" story if none of your anchors show growth.

---

## How many, and how polished

- **8–12 stories** total. Fewer and you'll have gaps; more and you won't know any of them deeply.
- **2–3 anchor stories** (multi-signal, your best impact) you know cold.
- Coverage check: every signal from Chapter 1 (ownership, impact, collaboration/conflict, ambiguity, growth, communication) should be hit by **at least two** stories, so you're never stuck if one doesn't fit.
- Polished means **written and rehearsed out loud**, not memorized word-for-word. You want to know the *beats*, then speak naturally (Chapter 10).

> 💡 **Concept notes — the coverage matrix**
> Make a simple grid: stories as rows, the six signals as columns, a checkmark where a story can deliver a signal. Scan the columns — any column with fewer than two checks is a **gap**; go find or build a story for it. This grid (a worked version is in the appendix) is the single most useful artifact of your prep: it turns "am I ready?" from a feeling into a check. Bring it to your final review and confirm every column has at least two marks.

## Try it

1. Set a 30-minute timer and write *raw* one-line notes for as many of the twelve mining prompts above as you can. Don't polish — just capture the memory.
2. Pick your single best project and list *every* signal-bearing moment in it (initiative, conflict, mistake, impact...). Aim for at least three — this is an anchor-story candidate.
3. Fill out the full story-bank template (appendix) for your two strongest stories, including the "what would you do differently?" field.
4. Draw the coverage matrix for the stories you have so far. Which signal column is emptiest? That's your next story to build.

*Write your answers in [behavioural-chapter-3-tryit.md](behavioural-chapter-3-tryit.md).*

## The bumper sticker

> *You're not prepping a hundred answers — you're building eight to twelve stories, tagged by the signals they deliver, with the numbers and follow-ups written down. Build two or three multi-signal anchors, cover every signal twice, and any question maps to a card you already hold.*

That completes the toolkit. Part 2 takes the bank you've built and aims it — one question family at a time, starting with the most common and most fumbled: conflict and disagreement.
