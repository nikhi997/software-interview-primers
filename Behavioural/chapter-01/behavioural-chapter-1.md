# Chapter 1: The question behind the question

*[Contents](../behavioural-README.md)*

- [ ] **Mark as read**

You walk into the behavioural round relieved — no algorithms, no whiteboard, just "tell me about yourself" and a few stories. It feels like the easy part. Then weeks later the recruiter says you were strong technically but "didn't show enough leadership signal," and you're baffled, because you *answered every question.*

Here's what happened: you heard the questions literally. The interviewer wasn't asking them literally. This chapter is about the gap between what's asked and what's being scored — because every behavioural question is a probe for a specific signal, and once you see the signal, the round stops being mysterious.

---

## The literal question is a decoy

Consider: *"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate."*

The naive read: *they want to hear about a disagreement.* So you describe a time you were right and your teammate was wrong, you stuck to your guns, and you won. Clean story, you think.

You just failed the question. Because the disagreement was never the point. The interviewer is scoring: *Can this person disagree without being difficult? Do they actually listen to other views, or just bulldoze? Do they escalate destructively or resolve collaboratively? When they're wrong, do they notice?* Your "I was right and I won" story signals the opposite of all of that — it reads as inflexible and unreflective.

> 💡 **Concept notes — the signal vs the surface**
> Every behavioural question has two layers: the **surface** (the literal anecdote it asks for) and the **signal** (the trait it's measuring). Interviewers are trained to ask open surface questions precisely because they reveal signal you can't fake — *what you choose to emphasize* exposes how you actually operate. The entire skill of behavioural interviewing is: hear the surface question, identify the signal, and tell a story that delivers *that signal* while answering the surface honestly. The rest of this book is signal-recognition drills.

---

## What's actually on the rubric

After you leave, the interviewer fills out a form. It doesn't say "good story / bad story." It has named dimensions, scored, with evidence. Across companies the labels differ but they cluster into six recurring signals:

| Signal | The question it secretly answers |
|---|---|
| **Ownership / drive** | "Will they push past obstacles without being told to?" |
| **Impact** | "Does their work actually move the needle — and do they know if it did?" |
| **Collaboration / conflict** | "Is this someone the team will want to work with?" |
| **Ambiguity** | "Can they make progress when the problem is underspecified?" |
| **Growth / self-awareness** | "Do they learn from mistakes, or repeat them?" |
| **Communication** | "Can they explain their thinking clearly and honestly?" |

> 💡 **Concept notes — "Leadership Principles" and their cousins**
> Many companies formalize these signals into a named framework. Amazon, for example, publishes its [Leadership Principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles), including Customer Obsession, Ownership, Dive Deep, and Disagree and Commit. The vocabulary changes from company to company, but the underlying signals barely do. If you're interviewing at a company with a published framework, **read it and map your stories to its exact terms** — the stories you build in this book will still translate.

---

## Why "I" matters more than "we"

A subtle, fatal habit: engineers describe team accomplishments in the first-person plural. *"We redesigned the pipeline, we cut latency, we shipped it."* It sounds humble and collaborative. It's also useless to the interviewer, because they can't tell **what *you* did.**

The behavioural round scores *individuals*. When you say "we," the signal goes to zero — the interviewer has no idea whether you led the redesign or fetched coffee. You must, slightly uncomfortably, say **"I":** *"I noticed the bottleneck, I proposed the redesign, I convinced the team, I implemented the caching layer."* Credit the team where it's due — that itself signals collaboration — but the *spine* of every story is your specific actions.

> 💡 **Concept notes — the "I/we" balance**
> Use **"we"** to set the scene and to credit others (it signals you're not a glory-hog). Use **"I"** for every decision, action, and judgment call you personally made — that's the data the rubric needs. A good story is mostly "I" verbs (*I investigated, I decided, I built, I escalated, I followed up*) wrapped in enough "we" to show you're a team player. If someone listening can't list three things *you specifically* did, the story has failed regardless of how impressive the outcome.

---

## Impact: the number that makes a story real

The difference between a forgettable story and a strong one is almost always a **number.** "I improved performance" is a claim. "I cut p99 latency from 800ms to 120ms, which dropped our checkout abandonment by 4%" is *evidence* — and evidence is what fills the rubric.

You will not always have clean metrics, and that's fine — but you should always reach for the most concrete impact available: time saved, errors reduced, revenue/users affected, people unblocked, a deadline met that was at risk. Even "saved the on-call team ~5 hours a week" beats "made things better." Hunt for the number in *every* story you build.

> 💡 **Concept notes — quantifying impact when you have no metrics**
> No dashboard? Estimate honestly and say you're estimating: *"We didn't instrument it precisely, but it eliminated a manual step that took the team roughly half a day each week."* Other concrete-impact forms when hard numbers are missing: **scope** ("used by all four product teams"), **risk averted** ("prevented a data-loss bug from reaching production"), **unblocking** ("unblocked three downstream teams"), **adoption** ("became the standard approach for new services"). The goal is to make the impact *tangible*, not necessarily numeric.

## Try it

1. Take the question *"Tell me about a project you're proud of."* What are the *two or three signals* it's probably probing? (Hint: it's rarely just "are you proud.")
2. Think of a recent team accomplishment. Write two versions of one sentence about it — one all "we," one centered on "I." Notice how much more an interviewer learns from the second.
3. Pick one thing you built or fixed in the last year. What's the most concrete *number* you can attach to its impact? If you don't have one, what's the best non-numeric evidence (scope, risk, unblocking)?
4. If you're targeting a specific company, find its behavioural/leadership framework and list the named dimensions. Which two do you have the weakest stories for right now?

*Write your answers in [behavioural-chapter-1-tryit.md](behavioural-chapter-1-tryit.md).*
