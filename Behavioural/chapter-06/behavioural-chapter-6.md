# Chapter 6: Leadership, ownership, and initiative

*[← Chapter 5](../chapter-05/behavioural-chapter-5.md) · [Contents](../behavioural-README.md)*

- [ ] **Mark as read**

When an interviewer asks about leadership, engineers without a manager title often freeze: *"I've never led anyone — I don't have reports."* That misreads the question entirely. In engineering, leadership almost never means having authority. It means **driving an outcome that wasn't your assigned job, and bringing people along without being able to order them to follow.**

That kind of leadership — influence without authority — is exactly what separates a senior engineer from a competent one, and it's what this family is screening for. You have these stories; you just may not have labeled them as "leadership."

---

## The signal behind the question

Variants: *"Tell me about a time you showed leadership / took ownership / went beyond your role / drove a project / influenced others / took initiative."*

> 💡 **Concept notes — what leadership questions actually score**
> Three signals: (1) **Initiative / ownership** — do you act on problems you *notice*, even when they're not assigned, or wait to be told? (2) **Influence without authority** — can you get peers and seniors to follow a direction through persuasion, data, and trust rather than position? (3) **Follow-through** — do you drive things to *done*, including the unglamorous parts (coordination, follow-up, handling resistance)? Crucially, **a manager title is not required and barely relevant** — the interviewer wants evidence you *behave* like an owner. The strongest stories often come from people with no formal authority at all.

---

## Ownership: the "not my job" reflex, inverted

The clearest ownership signal is taking on a problem that was, technically, nobody's job — or at least not yours. Most people see an unowned problem and route around it. Owners see it as an opening.

> 💡 **Concept notes — "ownership" as a behavior, not a role**
> Ownership shows up as a sequence: you **notice** a problem others have normalized ("our flaky tests, our slow onboarding, our recurring incident"), you **take responsibility** for it without being asked, you **drive it to resolution** through the messy middle, and you **follow up** to make sure it stays fixed. The phrase that signals the *opposite* of ownership is "that wasn't my responsibility." The phrase that signals ownership is "no one owned it, so I did." Companies prize this so highly because owners scale — they fix things leaders never even have to notice.

---

## Influence without authority: the actual skill

The hard part of engineering leadership isn't deciding what to do — it's getting other smart, busy, opinionated people to go along when you can't *make* them. The mechanisms that work, and that you should show in your story:

- **Data and prototypes** — you don't argue, you *demonstrate*. A working proof-of-concept persuades better than any meeting.
- **Addressing concerns directly** — you find out why people are hesitant and engage with it (echoes the steelmanning from Chapter 4).
- **Building coalition incrementally** — you win over one respected skeptic, and the rest follow.
- **Making it easy to say yes** — you do the hard prep work so others just have to agree, not do.
- **Giving credit generously** — people follow those who share the win.

> 💡 **Concept notes — influence is earned, then spent**
> The best leadership stories show you *building* the credibility to influence (through competence and prior follow-through) and then *spending* it on a goal that helped the team. A senior engineer doesn't get followed because of their title — they get followed because they've been right before, they do what they say, and they make others successful. When you tell a leadership story, make the *mechanism* of influence explicit: not "I convinced everyone" but "I built a prototype, showed it to the most skeptical senior first, and once she was on board the rest of the team followed."

## A worked example

*Question: "Tell me about a time you took the lead on something."*

> **(S)** *Our team's on-call was miserable — the same three alert types fired every week, and everyone just acked them and moved on. It wasn't on anyone's roadmap; it was just accepted pain.* **(T)** *No one owned alert quality, but it was burning out the team, so I decided to own it even though it wasn't assigned to me.* **(A)** *I pulled three months of alert data and showed that 70% of pages came from those three causes, two of which were auto-recoverable and shouldn't have paged anyone. I knew I couldn't just mandate changes, so I took the noisiest alert, fixed its root cause myself as a proof of concept, and brought the before/after to our team meeting. That made it concrete, and people volunteered to take the other two. I set up a lightweight weekly review so new noisy alerts got caught early.* **(R)** *Pages dropped about 60% over two months, and our on-call satisfaction in the team survey went from the lowest on the org to the highest. I didn't have any authority to make people do this — what worked was showing the data, doing the first one myself, and letting the results recruit everyone else.*

No title, no mandate. Pure ownership (took unowned pain), influence without authority (data + lead-by-example + coalition), and follow-through (the weekly review that kept it fixed), with quantified impact. That's the template.

## Initiative: the "I noticed, so I" pattern

Initiative questions reward the same instinct in smaller form: you saw something that could be better and *acted* without being told. The narrative spine is literally "**I noticed X, so I did Y**" — and the strength comes from acting *before* anyone asked.

> 💡 **Concept notes — calibrating initiative to your level**
> Initiative scales with seniority, and that's fine. For a junior engineer, "I noticed our README was out of date and new hires kept getting stuck, so I rewrote the setup guide and cut onboarding from two days to two hours" is a perfectly strong initiative story. For a senior, the scope is larger — driving a cross-team migration, defining a new standard. Don't dismiss your story as "too small": the *behavior* (noticing, owning, acting) is what's scored, not the scope. Pick the biggest-scope true story you have, but a modest, real one beats an inflated, vague one.

---

## What sinks a leadership answer

- **"I've never led, I have no reports"** — a misread; reframe around driving outcomes and influencing peers.
- **All "we," no "I"** — the cardinal sin (Chapter 1). Leadership stories *especially* need your specific driving actions.
- **Authority as the mechanism** — "I told them to, so they did." Shows position, not leadership. The signal is influence *without* power.
- **No follow-through** — you started something but it fizzled or you handed it off and never checked. Drive to *done*.
- **No resistance overcome** — if everyone instantly agreed, where was the leadership? Show the skepticism you won over.
- **Taking sole credit** — claiming a team's work as entirely yours reads as the wrong kind of "leader." Credit others while owning your role.

## Try it

1. List three problems you've fixed that *weren't assigned to you.* Each is an ownership story. Which has the clearest before/after impact?
2. For your best leadership story, name the **mechanism of influence** explicitly: data? a prototype? winning over one key skeptic? leading by example? If you can't name it, the story isn't showing leadership yet — just activity.
3. Find a "leadership" story you have where you had *no authority at all.* That's usually your strongest card in this family — why?
4. Take a small initiative ("I noticed X, so I did Y") and check: does it show you acting *before* being asked, and following through to a result? Quantify the result.

## The bumper sticker

> *Engineering leadership isn't a title — it's driving an unowned outcome and bringing people along without authority. Show the problem you noticed, the influence mechanism (data, prototype, winning the skeptic), and the follow-through to done. "No one owned it, so I did" is the whole signal.*
